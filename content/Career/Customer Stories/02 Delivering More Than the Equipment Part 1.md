## **From First Contact to Completed Delivery**

### **A 19-Month Journey with a New Research Laboratory

## **Building a New Laboratory’s Testing Capability**

In the autumn of 2022, a Chinese Assistant Professor at a university in the Boston area had just started his new position and was building his own research group and laboratory.

At that time, I had been working in battery testing equipment sales for a little over one year. I was not new to international trade — before this role, I had worked in industrial lighting export sales. However, this was still one of my early experiences handling a complex industrial equipment case involving multiple systems, university procurement, a long production cycle, international logistics, and installation support.

In October 2022, I first contacted this newly established laboratory.

A few days later, one of the researchers from the lab replied.

The initial requirement was not simply about purchasing one piece of equipment. The laboratory needed testing capabilities for different types of batteries, including coin cells, cylindrical cells, small and large pouch cells, and battery modules. The solution involved different current and voltage ranges, temperature chambers, fixtures, and other configurations.

A new laboratory was gradually building the testing capability it would need for future research.

And this was where the journey began.

## **From Initial Requirements to a Complete Testing Solution**

Over the following weeks, we continued discussing the laboratory’s requirements and equipment configuration.

The initial ideas gradually developed into a more complete testing solution.

By December 2022, the professor had decided to work with our company.

However, a customer’s decision to purchase does not always mean the order immediately moves into production.

The university procurement process took several months.

During this period, the purchase order was temporarily returned, and I provided the supplier information required by the university’s finance department. At the same time, the professor’s new laboratory was still being built, and the laboratory team needed equipment information related to installation and power requirements.

So those months were not simply a waiting period.

On one side was the university procurement process.

On the other side was a new laboratory gradually taking shape.

In the spring of 2023, the professor finally sent me:

“Great news! Your PO is finally ready.”

By then, around six months had passed since our first communication.

However, even after the PO was released, the requirements were not completely fixed.

The laboratory continued confirming cables, clamps, and the actual usage configuration.

Later, the professor considered changing the model of one of the temperature chambers.

This change happened after the order process had already started.

The original chamber order needed to be cancelled, the new model needed to be applied for and reordered, and the production timeline had to be recalculated.

This became one of the factors that extended the overall delivery timeline.

The order was confirmed, but the laboratory itself was still developing, and the customer’s practical requirements continued to evolve.

The customer originally hoped to receive the equipment in early September 2023.

However, after the temperature chamber change, the original production and delivery schedule was affected.

Based on the transportation timeline at that time, completing production earlier was necessary if we still wanted to maintain the original delivery expectation. Therefore, before the new chamber model was fully confirmed, I had already informed the customer that this change could affect the original schedule.

Later, the new chamber model was confirmed and the order entered a new production process. The delivery timeline was recalculated accordingly.

As summer passed, the original early September arrival date became increasingly difficult to achieve.

The customer asked for updated ETAs, and I continued checking the production status internally and sharing the latest confirmed information.

Eventually, the original delivery plan could not be maintained.

There was no simple “fix” for the delay.

The customer’s requirements had changed, production required additional time, and international transportation also required time.

The process kept moving forward, and communication continued throughout the changes.

## **When Commercial Terms and Customer Expectations Were Not Exactly the Same**

As the equipment was approaching completion and preparation for shipment began, another experience left a strong impression on me.

My first job was industrial lighting export sales.

At that time, many of my customers were professional purchasers, and terms such as EXW and FOB were part of our daily business communication.

After joining the battery testing equipment industry, many of my customers became professors and researchers.

They were experts in their own research fields, but international trade terms were not part of their daily work.

The original quotation for this laboratory was based on EXW.

Later, when I received the university PO, I noticed that it stated FOB.

I explained the difference to the customer: the original quotation was EXW, while FOB meant we would be responsible for delivering the equipment to the port in Shenzhen, which would involve additional costs.

The professor understood and agreed to handle the additional payment.

At that time, I thought the matter had been clearly explained.

However, when we moved closer to the actual shipment arrangement, I realized something important:

Even though we had discussed EXW and FOB, and the responsibility boundaries had been explained, we did not necessarily have exactly the same picture in our minds of what the final delivery process would look like.

From my perspective, I was thinking about:

What responsibilities does this trade term define?

From the professor’s perspective, the more practical question was:

How will these machines actually arrive at my laboratory?

There was no misunderstanding caused by one side being right and the other being wrong.

For a professor building a new research laboratory, it was completely natural that his focus was on whether the equipment could successfully arrive and be ready for research use.

This experience taught me something important:

Explaining a commercial term correctly does not always mean both sides have the same understanding of the final outcome.

When working with different types of customers, sales should not only ask:

“Have I explained the term clearly?”

but also:

“Do we have the same picture of what will happen next?”

## **From Shipment to Laboratory Operation**

After recognizing this difference, I continued arranging the transportation plan for the equipment to the Boston laboratory.

Because the university PO only covered the equipment cost, the additional transportation expenses needed to be handled separately. The professor applied for alternative payment arrangements for those additional costs.

The equipment was gradually completed, but the delivery process was still not finished.

Time continued moving forward.

Production was finally completed in the autumn of 2023.

Then came international transportation.

The equipment spent more than two months at sea.

In December 2023, more than one year had passed since our first communication.

On Christmas Day, the professor informed me that they had received:

“8 crates of battery cyclers and chambers.”

More than a year earlier, this was a newly established laboratory discussing future testing requirements.

Now, eight crates containing testing equipment and temperature chambers had finally arrived in Boston.

But receiving the equipment did not mean the laboratory could immediately start using it.

In early 2024, I continued arranging remote support from our service engineers to assist with installation.

The laboratory gradually began operating the equipment.

At the same time, the additional transportation payment outside the original PO was still being processed.

By May 2024, all related payments had been completed.

## **Beyond Delivery**

From the first contact in October 2022 to requirement discussions, procurement, production, changes, transportation, delivery, installation, and final settlement:

approximately 19 months had passed.

Looking back, there was no single dramatic moment during these 19 months.

Following up on production progress was not difficult.

Handling a configuration change was not difficult.

Confirming transportation arrangements was not difficult.

Arranging remote installation was not something extraordinary either.

But when these ordinary tasks continue across months, then a year, and eventually into the second year, the weight of the journey gradually becomes visible.

The challenge of many industrial B2B cases is not always one extremely complicated problem.

It is whether someone continues to carry the responsibility forward after time has passed.

This experience changed how I view delivery.

Delivering a solution is not only about completing production or receiving a PO.

For this laboratory, the real outcome was that the equipment crossed the ocean, arrived at the laboratory, was installed successfully, and became part of their actual research work.

In May 2024, the journey that started in the autumn of 2022 seemed to have reached its first milestone.

But once the equipment started running, the story with this laboratory entered another stage.
