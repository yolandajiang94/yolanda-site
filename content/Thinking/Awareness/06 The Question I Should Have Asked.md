# **The Question I Should Have Asked**

For a long time, whenever I faced something painful or difficult, I naturally searched for explanations.

I asked:

Why did this happen?

Why did someone behave this way?

Why did things become like this?

Sometimes, I even asked:

**Why did I allow this to happen?**

I believed that if I could fully understand the reason behind something, I would finally be able to move on.

But later, I realized that some questions, although they look like they are helping us find answers, can actually keep us trapped in the past.

---

## **A Question That Kept Me Looking Back**

“Why did I allow this to happen?”

At first, it seemed like a question about understanding.

But underneath it, there was another question:

What was wrong with me?

Why did I not notice earlier?

Why did I not make a better choice?

Why did I not handle it differently?

Without realizing it, this question often led me toward self-blame.

Instead of helping me move forward, it kept me reviewing the past again and again.

I was trying to find the moment when things went wrong.

I was trying to find the reason why I did not do better.

---

## **A Different Question**

Later, I learned to ask a different question:

**Why did I not stop it?**

At first, these two questions seemed almost identical.

But gradually, I realized they came from completely different perspectives.

“Why did I allow this to happen?”

focuses on:

What happened?

Why did it happen?

Who caused it?

It keeps my attention on the past.

But:

“Why did I not stop it?”

focuses on:

When I already felt uncomfortable, what kept me there?

What prevented me from taking action?

What made me ignore my own boundaries?

This question does not deny what happened.

It does not mean I am responsible for everything.

Life is complicated, and many situations are shaped by many different factors.

But this question brings my attention back to something I still have:

My choices.

---

## **From Explaining the Past to Recognizing My Agency**

This was a significant shift in the way I think.

In the past, I often tried to understand everything first.

I wanted to know:

Why did this happen?

Why did I react this way?

Why did I make that choice?

Understanding myself was important.

But I gradually realized:

Understanding is not the final destination.

The more important question is:

**What can I do now?**

---

Looking back at the past year, I noticed that many of my reflections followed the same pattern.

Before, I often asked:

Why did I lose my direction?

Why did I delay taking action?

Why did I spend so much energy on things that were not truly important?

Why did things become this way?

These questions helped me see my patterns.

But seeing patterns is only the beginning.

The next step is learning how to respond differently.

---

## **The Same Question Appears Everywhere**

When I think about my career, the question is no longer:

Why did I not make better choices earlier?

It becomes:

What kind of choice fits me now?

When facing a difficult task, the question is no longer:

Why do I always feel overwhelmed?

It becomes:

What is the next step I can take?

When I notice old patterns returning, the question is no longer:

Why am I like this?

It becomes:

What can I choose differently this time?

---

## **From Understanding to Action**

I used to believe that I needed complete understanding before taking action.

I wanted:

A clear explanation before making a decision.

A complete answer before moving forward.

Enough certainty before changing direction.

But many important things in life do not work that way.

Some answers only appear after we take action.

Some changes only happen after we make a choice.

---

This has become one of the biggest changes in my thinking.

I still value reflection.

I still want to understand the deeper reasons behind things.

But I am learning:

Understanding should not become a reason to stay still.

Reflection should eventually lead to choice.

And choice should eventually lead to action.

---

A better question can change the direction of our lives.

Sometimes, change does not begin with finding a new answer.

It begins with asking a different question.

From:

**“Why did I allow this to happen?”**

To:

**“Why did I not stop it?”**

This shift does not erase the past.

But it changes my relationship with the past.

Instead of only looking at what happened to me, I begin to look at what I can do next.

And that is where change begins.
