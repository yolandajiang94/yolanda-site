# **Why I Started Before I Felt Ready**

For a long time, I thought the reason I struggled to start was that I needed more preparation.

I told myself:

“I just need to think more clearly.”

“I just need a better plan.”

“When everything is ready, I will begin.”

This way of thinking made me feel responsible and careful.

But over time, I realized that preparation was not always helping me move forward.

Sometimes, it became a way to delay action.

---

## **My Old Pattern of Starting**

When facing something important, my first instinct was always to build a complete understanding before taking the first step.

I wanted to know:

What exactly is this task?

What are all the possible challenges?

What should the final result look like?

How can I avoid making mistakes?

This mindset helped me in many situations.

It made me thoughtful and cautious.

But it also created another problem:

The more important something was, the more complicated it became in my mind.

And the more complicated it seemed, the harder it was to start.

It was not because I was lazy.

It was because starting meant facing uncertainty.

---

## **When Preparation Became a Barrier**

Looking back, I realize that I often confused preparation with responsibility.

I believed:

“If I want to do something well, I should first understand everything.”

But sometimes, excessive preparation was simply a way to avoid the discomfort of taking action.

Thinking feels safe.

Planning feels safe.

Researching feels safe.

Because in these stages, I do not have to face failure or unexpected problems.

But action is different.

Once I start, reality begins to provide feedback.

I have to adjust.

I have to make decisions.

I have to accept that things may not go exactly as planned.

For a long time, I wanted clarity before action.

But many things only become clear through action.

---

## **Learning to Start with My Career Transition**

This pattern became especially obvious when I started preparing for my next career stage.

One example was my resume.

The last time I seriously looked for a job was five years ago.

After working for several years, I felt that my resume should represent my experience properly.

It should be mature.

It should clearly show my capabilities.

It should reflect what I had learned from my career.

Because of this, creating a resume became much bigger in my mind than it actually was.

I did not know where to start.

So I delayed it for several days.

---

Later, when I finally started, I realized it was not as difficult as I imagined.

I did not create a perfect resume in one day.

I simply moved forward step by step.

On the first day, I listed:

· my employment history;

· the main responsibilities of each role;

· important projects and experiences.

On the second day, I reviewed and improved the descriptions.

I thought about:

Which experiences showed my strengths?

Which achievements were worth highlighting?

On the third day, I selected a suitable resume format and organized everything into the final version.

The whole process took several days.

But each day, I only spent around half an hour.

In the end, a resume that I was satisfied with was completed.

The task did not become smaller.

But the next step became clearer.

---

## **The Same Experience with Interview Preparation**

Preparing STAR interview stories was another example.

I knew early that STAR examples were important for interviews.

But I postponed it for a long time.

The reason was simple.

Those cases came from real work experiences.

Many of them involved complicated customer requirements, internal coordination, and difficult decisions.

At the time, solving those problems already required a lot of energy.

Looking back at them meant:

Reviewing the whole situation.

Organizing the timeline.

Finding the lessons behind the experience.

It felt like another difficult project.

So I avoided starting.

---

Then one day, I changed my approach.

Instead of telling myself:

“I need to write a perfect STAR story.”

I simply wrote down everything I remembered about one case and shared the details.

What happened.

What difficulties I faced.

What I did.

What the result was.

By organizing the information step by step, the story gradually became clear.

I realized that many things felt overwhelming because I defined them in a much harder way than necessary.

Writing a STAR story sounded difficult.

But collecting my experiences and organizing them was much easier.

The first step changed the whole process.

---

## **The Same Pattern in Daily Work**

I also noticed this pattern when dealing with complex customer requests.

When a difficult requirement came in, my first reaction was sometimes feeling overwhelmed.

My mind immediately jumped to:

How can I solve this?

How many problems are involved?

What if I cannot handle it?

Sometimes, I would unconsciously turn to easier tasks instead.

But later, I learned that complex problems rarely need to be solved completely at the beginning.

The first step is often enough:

Understand the customer's basic requirement.

Identify the key questions.

Communicate with internal teams.

Then move forward from there.

The hardest part was usually not solving the problem.

It was starting.

---

## **Building My Digital Garden Step by Step**

Creating my personal website was another example.

Before I started, I thought building a website was a huge project.

A domain name.

A website framework.

The structure.

The content.

The design.

Everything seemed unfamiliar.

If I looked at the whole picture, it felt like a technical project that required a lot of knowledge.

But after I started, I realized it was simply a series of small steps.

After purchasing my domain, I gradually built it:

First, I defined the website direction.

Then, I designed the structure.

Then, I learned how to set up Quartz.

Then, I adjusted the layout and started writing.

The website was not created in one day.

It grew through small actions over time.

---

## **Changing the Way I Learn**

I also experienced the same change in learning English.

For a long time, English learning felt like an endless task.

There was no clear finish line.

Because of that, it was easy to feel pressure but difficult to know where to begin.

Later, I started dividing it into smaller areas:

· workplace communication;

· daily conversations;

· vocabulary building;

· speaking practice.

Once the task became more specific, it became easier to take action.

---

## **What I Learned**

Looking back, I realize that my biggest obstacle was not the difficulty of the tasks themselves.

It was my expectation that I needed to fully understand everything before I started.

I wanted:

clarity before action.

confidence before action.

a complete plan before action.

But life does not always work that way.

Many things become clear only after we start.

I am learning that action is not the result of complete certainty.

Sometimes, action is the way we create certainty.

---

**Many things become clear only after we start.**
