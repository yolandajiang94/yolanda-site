# **From Having Experience to Understanding It**

For years, I knew that experience was something worth reflecting on.

I could feel that I understood customers better than when I first started working. I had handled more projects, dealt with more problems, and become more comfortable with situations that once felt difficult.

But knowing that reflection matters and actually making time for it are two different things.

In daily work, there is always something more immediate to do.

A customer needs a reply.  
A project needs to move forward.  
An order needs to be followed up.  
A problem needs to be solved.

Once one thing is finished, the next one is already waiting.

So for a long time, much of my experience accumulated in a simple way:

**Something happened. I dealt with it. Then I moved on.**

I knew I was gaining experience, but much of what I learned remained scattered and attached to individual situations. I rarely stopped long enough to ask what exactly I had learned from them.

## **Stepping Outside the Flow of Daily Work**

This started to change gradually over the past several months.

There was no single moment when I suddenly realized that I should reflect on my career. The awareness had always been there.

What changed was that I began to spend more time stepping outside the flow of daily work and looking back.

Using GPT made this easier. It did not give meaning to my experience for me, but it lowered the effort required to organize things that had been scattered across my memory, old files, notes, and conversations.

Instead of only asking:

**What should I do next?**

I started asking different questions:

What actually happened here?

Why did I handle it that way?

What judgment did I make?

What did this experience leave me with?

That difference sounds small, but it changed the way I looked at my own work.

I was no longer only going through experiences. I was beginning to examine them.

## **Looking at an Experience More Closely**

One practical example came when I was preparing STAR stories during my job search.

This was only one small part of a much longer period of reflection, but it gave me a surprisingly strict way to examine past projects.

A successful order was not enough.

I had to identify the situation, the actual problem, what I needed to accomplish, what I personally did, and how those actions affected the result.

That distinction mattered.

Over the years, I had completed many orders. But not every successful order contained a clear example of my own judgment.

Sometimes a customer already knew the company and its products. They had a clear requirement, contacted me when they were ready to purchase, and the order moved forward.

It was a real sale and a real result.

But when I asked myself:

**What important judgment did I actually make in this case?**

Sometimes there was no particularly strong answer.

The projects that became useful STAR stories were usually the more important or complicated ones, where I could clearly identify the problem I faced, the information available at the time, the judgment I made, the actions I took, and what happened afterward.

Breaking those projects down helped me see a structure that I had rarely made explicit before:

**Problem → Information → Judgment → Action → Result**

I had lived through those projects before.

But living through an experience and understanding what happened inside it are not the same thing.

## **From Remembering an Experience to Understanding It**

In the past, when I looked back at my work, I often remembered events.

I worked with this customer.

I handled that problem.

That project was complicated.

That order eventually went through.

Those memories are part of experience, but I now think there is another layer underneath them.

Why was the project difficult?

What was the real problem?

What information did I have at the time?

What did I decide, and why?

Which of my actions actually affected what happened next?

If I faced the same situation again, would I make the same decision?

Did anything I learned change the way I handled a similar situation later?

These questions turn a memory into something more useful.

An experience becomes part of my history simply because it happened.

But it begins to become part of my understanding only when I can make sense of what happened and why.

That is also why reflection has become more important to me.

I do not want to document every project or write a lesson after every working day. Most things do not need that level of attention.

But some experiences contain something worth keeping.

If I never return to them, what remains after a few years may be little more than a vague memory that I once dealt with something difficult.

## **Why I Keep a Digital Garden**

This is also one reason my digital garden has gradually become more meaningful to me.

Over the years, useful thoughts have ended up everywhere: in work files, temporary notes, conversations, and my own memory.

I do not want to preserve all of them.

A digital garden, to me, is not a record of everything that happens in my work or life. Nor is every new realization ready to become an article.

What I want to keep are the smaller number of ideas that survive reflection, continue to make sense over time, and become clearer through real experience.

The process is less about recording more and more about understanding what is worth recording:

**Experience → Looking Back → Breaking It Down → Understanding → Keeping What Matters**

The writing comes at the end, not the beginning.

## **Experience Is Only the Beginning**

I still believe that years of real work matter.

There is no substitute for dealing with actual customers, projects, uncertainty, mistakes, and results.

But I no longer assume that simply going through more situations means I will automatically understand them better.

Experience gives me the material.

Reflection helps me see what is actually inside it.

The change I am trying to make is simple: instead of letting one experience disappear into the next, I want to occasionally stop, look back, and understand what really happened.

Not every experience will leave behind an important lesson. But when one does, I want to take the time to understand what it taught me before moving on.
