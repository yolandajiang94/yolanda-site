Some B2B projects are difficult because the technical requirements are complicated.

Others are difficult for a much simpler reason: **the customer is in a hurry.**

I had one of those experiences while handling an equipment order for a research team at a university in South Korea.

The equipment itself was a standard product, and the delivery eventually went smoothly. What made the project memorable was the communication along the way.

My main contact was a postdoctoral researcher. From very early in the project, he had one clear priority:

**He wanted the equipment as soon as possible.**

I understood that.

But wanting something quickly and being ready to purchase it are not always the same thing.

## **The Urgency Started Early**

After the professor introduced us, the researcher quickly moved our communication to WeChat because it was more convenient for him.

We confirmed the basic specifications and discussed pricing, service, and delivery.

Then the questions started coming.

When could we ship?

Could we provide the tax invoice?

Could the equipment arrive sooner?

The interesting part was that these questions started before the purchasing process itself was fully ready.

At one point, we were still waiting for a purchase order. After several rounds of clarification, I realized that the university would not issue a standard PO for this particular purchase.

That changed the situation.

The question was no longer:

**When will the PO arrive?**

It became:

**What formal documentation can we use instead so that the order can move forward properly?**

I confirmed the requirements internally, communicated with the university by email, obtained the necessary confirmation from the professor, and applied internally to proceed without a standard PO.

Because this was an exception to our usual ordering process, it required additional internal approval before the order could formally move forward.

The customer was still asking about shipment and the tax invoice.

But before either of those could happen, there were still steps we needed to complete.

## **“What About the Tax Invoice?”**

The tax invoice became almost a running theme throughout the project.

My contact started asking about it quite early—long before we had reached the stage when it could actually be issued.

I explained the process, but the question would come back again.

From his perspective, it made sense. He had a few things he knew he needed:

the equipment, the shipment, and the tax invoice.

He wanted all of them as soon as possible.

From my side, however, I had to separate **what the customer ultimately wanted** from **what could actually be done at that moment**.

Throughout the project, I kept coming back to four questions:

**Where is the order actually at right now?**

**What needs to happen before we can move to the next step?**

**What can I prepare in advance?**

**What cannot be done yet?**

Those questions became the structure behind how I managed the order.

## **Moving the Order Forward, Step by Step**

Once we had found a workable purchasing route, there was still a long way to go before the equipment could simply be “shipped.”

The order first had to be formally entered into our internal system. It then had to move through several levels of internal review and approval. Because there was no standard PO, this order required an additional approval process as well.

Only after those requirements were completed could the order be formally released.

From there, I continued following each stage:

·  internal approval and order release

·  equipment preparation and final inspection

·  packing and shipment

·  international transportation and customs clearance

·  and finally local delivery

I kept the customer updated at key stages throughout the process.

This was important because the customer often wanted to jump directly to the final question:

**When will I receive the equipment?**

But my job was to keep translating that question into the next step we could actually complete.

I was not trying to slow the process down.

I was trying to move it forward without pretending that steps which had not happened yet were already complete.

## **Managing Expectations Without Slowing Things Down**

The researcher continued asking when the equipment would ship and when it would arrive.

I knew the timeline mattered to him, so wherever something could be prepared or accelerated, I pushed it forward.

At the same time, I avoided giving him certainty that I did not yet have.

Instead of responding to urgency with another promise, I responded with progress:

**This is where we are now.**

**This is what has just been completed.**

**This is what comes next.**

That gave the customer visibility into a process that he could not see from his side.

More importantly, it kept the project moving according to its actual status rather than according to the pressure of the latest message.

Eventually, the shipment progressed faster than my contact expected.

When I told him that the equipment had already completed customs clearance in South Korea, his reaction was immediate:

“Oh really? Already in Korea?”

After repeatedly asking when the equipment would arrive, he seemed genuinely surprised that it was already there.

In the end, we met his expected timeline—and even moved ahead of it.

## **Urgency Is Not the Same as Readiness**

Looking back, this was not a technically difficult order. There was no major customization, serious product problem, or difficult negotiation.

But it was a useful reminder that a customer's urgency does not always mean the process is ready to move at the same speed. My job was to understand what had already been completed, what was blocking the next step, and what could actually be moved forward.

That allowed me to give the customer something more useful than reassurance: a clear picture of where the order actually stood and what was happening next.

In simple terms:

**I know you're in a hurry. Let's get this step right first.**
