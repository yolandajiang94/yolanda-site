
A U.S. industrial customer urgently needed a high-power testing system. To receive it as soon as possible, they requested air shipment. Given the value of the equipment, we purchased transportation insurance before dispatch. On May 29, the system was shipped in a wooden crate.

On June 2, while it was still in transit, I reminded the customer to inspect both the crate and the equipment upon delivery. If there was any shipping damage, I asked them to take photos, obtain a damage record from the carrier if possible, and inform us quickly so that the insurance process could be started.

At the time, this seemed like a routine precaution. A few days later, it became an important part of a much more complicated situation.

# **The equipment arrived damaged**

On June 10, the customer emailed me to say that the system had arrived with visible physical damage.

They sent a large number of photos. There was damage to the wooden crate, deformation on parts of the equipment, and a component had come loose inside the system. The customer also explained that the crate had been wrapped in plastic film, which made some of the external damage difficult to notice when the shipment was first received.

This exposed a practical problem I have encountered more than once in industrial equipment deliveries.

In theory, the rule is straightforward: inspect the shipment at the time of delivery and document any visible damage before or when signing for it.

In practice, large industrial equipment is not a consumer parcel. A system may arrive inside a wooden crate that requires tools, space, and the right personnel to open. The person receiving the shipment may not be the engineer who will eventually install and use it. In many cases, the shipment is signed for first and unpacked later—sometimes days after delivery.

That gap between formal receiving procedures and the reality of an industrial site would make this case much harder to resolve.

# **The first response**

As soon as the customer reported the damage, I informed the company and sent the photos to our engineering and after-sales teams.

Based on the images, their preliminary view was that there was no obvious damage to the core internal components, while the visible damage appeared to be concentrated around the external structure. They suggested powering on the system to determine whether its functions had also been affected.

The customer declined. From their perspective, a visibly damaged high-power system represented a potential safety risk. They did not want their employees installing, powering on, or further testing it.

It was an understandable boundary. The customer was otherwise highly cooperative throughout the case, providing photos, videos, documents, and later working directly with the insurance investigation. They simply did not believe that powering on visibly damaged high-power equipment was a reasonable risk to take.

Because transportation insurance had been purchased before shipment, the company's direction at this stage was to work through our freight forwarder and continue with the carrier and insurance process. Following that direction, I stayed in contact with the customer and the freight forwarder as the claim developed.

# **The process kept moving**

The customer opened a claim with the international carrier. It was denied.

They continued to challenge the decision. The case was reopened, more photos were submitted, and the claim was reviewed again. It was denied again.

Meanwhile, our freight forwarder was communicating with the insurer about the transportation insurance we had purchased. As the investigation progressed, the insurance side began communicating directly with the customer and requesting additional evidence, including pre-shipment photos, damage photos, videos, and other documentation.

The customer continued to cooperate.

On July 6, an insurance representative even visited the customer's site in the United States to inspect the damaged shipment in person. A report was later submitted and the process continued.

For weeks, there was activity on all sides. The customer was providing information. I was communicating with the customer and following up with the freight forwarder. The freight forwarder was working with the insurer. The insurance process had progressed as far as an on-site inspection.

And yet one thing had not changed:

**The customer still did not have a usable system.**

**The process was moving, but the customer's problem was not being resolved.**

# **The customer asked for a replacement**

By mid-June, the customer had already asked us to take back the damaged system and provide a replacement. I reported this request to the company.

But replacing a high-value system was not a decision I could make myself. At that stage, the carrier and insurance processes were still active, and responsibility for the damage remained unclear. The company continued to watch how those channels developed while I handled the day-to-day communication with the customer and freight forwarder.

Sometimes there was a clear next action. At other times, the next step depended on the carrier or insurer, and we had to wait for new information.

# **The situation changed in August**

By August, the damaged equipment had been unresolved for almost two months.

On August 9, the customer wrote:

_“I need this resolved quickly.”_

They also made the commercial impact explicit:

_“We are not placing another PO until this is resolved.”_

The issue was no longer only an open after-sales case. It was beginning to affect the broader customer relationship.

On August 12, the customer told me that the international carrier had closed its case.

Then, on August 22, the customer's engineering director contacted me directly. He summarized the situation clearly: the equipment had been delivered damaged on June 10; the customer had provided the requested photos, videos, and other information; they would not power on the damaged system because of the safety concern; and after approximately ten weeks, they still did not have a workable solution.

They wanted to return the damaged system and receive a replacement.

The request itself was not new. What had changed was the weight of the situation. The normal carrier and insurance processes had now been running for more than two months without producing a practical solution. The carrier had closed its case. The unresolved issue was affecting future business. And now the customer's engineering director had formally stepped in after approximately ten weeks.

I reported this latest escalation to the company.

After reviewing the situation, management approved the production of a replacement system and asked me to arrange it as soon as possible. The normal production cycle for this type of equipment was around three months, but the replacement was expedited.

# **A practical resolution**

By the end of September, the customer had helped arrange the return of the damaged system.

In early October, the replacement finished production and testing. After the National Day holiday, the new system was shipped.

The case that had started with a damaged delivery in June finally reached a practical resolution several months later.

# **What stayed with me**

This case gave me a more realistic understanding of receiving procedures for industrial equipment.

It is still important to remind customers to inspect high-value shipments as early as practical, photograph any damage, and preserve carrier records. But large industrial systems are often delivered in wooden crates, and customers cannot always unpack and inspect them while the delivery driver is waiting.

There is a real gap between standard shipping procedures and the practical reality of industrial receiving. When a case falls into that gap, even an insured shipment can become difficult to resolve.

And when the normal process no longer produces a practical solution, sales may not have the final answer—or the authority to make the final decision.

**But someone still has to keep things moving toward a solution.**
