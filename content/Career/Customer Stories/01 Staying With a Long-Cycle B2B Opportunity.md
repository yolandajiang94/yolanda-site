In industrial B2B sales, many opportunities do not move quickly.

A customer may have a real need, but the purchasing timeline can remain uncertain for months. Budgets, internal procedures, changing requirements, and external conditions can all affect when a project moves forward.

One project I worked on with an overseas research organization lasted from early discussions in March until the formal procurement stage near the end of the year.

Looking back, I would not describe it as a project won through sophisticated sales techniques. The customer already had a real need, and part of the opportunity was simply being connected to the right research group when that need appeared.

What mattered was something more ordinary: staying with the opportunity, checking rather than assuming, keeping in touch without pushing, and adjusting my judgment when new information emerged.

## **Staying With an Opportunity Without a Clear Timeline**

The project started in March, when a researcher contacted me for a quotation on technical equipment.

There was clear interest, but no definite purchasing timeline. Over the following weeks, we communicated several times, and I provided updated quotations as the requirement developed.

There was no dramatic breakthrough. The project simply remained open.

I continued to keep track of the project rather than treating the absence of an immediate order as the end of the opportunity.

**In April, trade conditions between China and the United States changed rapidly, with tariffs becoming an increasing concern.**

It would have been easy to assume that the rising cost and uncertainty would cause the customer to stop the project.

But that would have been my assumption, not the customer's decision.

Instead, I contacted the customer and asked whether the requirement was still moving forward.

The answer was yes.

External conditions had changed; the customer's intention had not.

During July and August, there was still no clear procurement timeline. I stayed in touch occasionally, without pushing for a decision, simply keeping the communication open while the opportunity remained active.

## **When the Opportunity Started Moving Again**

By October, another researcher had become my main contact for the project.

I did not know the exact reason for the change, and there was no need to make assumptions about the organization's internal responsibilities. The underlying requirement was still active.

I continued the discussion with the new contact and provided an updated quotation. From my side, what mattered was maintaining continuity as the communication shifted from one person to another.

Near the end of the year, the researcher told me that someone from the procurement team would contact me.

I waited, but the expected communication did not arrive.

Instead of waiting indefinitely, I went back to the researcher and asked about the procurement progress. The researcher followed up internally, and the procurement team subsequently contacted me about the formal purchasing process.

Sometimes, long-cycle follow-up is simply about noticing when an expected next step has not happened and checking what is holding it up.

## **When Procurement Created an Ownership Question**

The procurement team contacted both me and another salesperson in my company.

This created an internal question of opportunity ownership.

I had been following the current requirement since March, communicating with the researchers and providing quotations for most of the year. I therefore had a reasonable basis for protecting my involvement in the project.

Rather than simply stepping aside or trying to settle the issue through a personal argument, I submitted the situation for an internal review.

Our legal team checked the records in the company's internal system and arranged a discussion with both of us.

The review revealed information I had not previously known.

My colleague had sold the same type of equipment to the organization before and was familiar with its formal procurement process. I also learned that previous projects had established commercial arrangements and configuration details that I had not been aware of when preparing my earlier quotations.

The legal team suggested that we work together.

With the fuller picture, collaboration made sense. This was no longer simply a new opportunity that I had been following since March; it was also part of an existing customer history that needed to be taken into account.

Based on those findings, I agreed that collaboration was the right outcome for this particular case.

That did not mean ownership was unimportant or that the work I had done no longer mattered. Bringing the issue to a formal review was part of protecting the value of that work.

But protecting my position and defending it regardless of new information are different things.

Once the previous customer history and procurement experience became clear, I was willing to adjust my judgment.

## **What Persistence Looked Like in This Project**

Looking back, this was not a story about one brilliant sales decision.

The customer had a genuine requirement, and I happened to connect with the right research group when that requirement emerged.

What I could control was how I responded as the situation changed.

None of these actions were extraordinary. But long sales cycles are often shaped by ordinary decisions made at the right time.

For me, persistence in industrial B2B sales is not about following up more often or holding on to every opportunity at any cost.

**It is about staying close enough to notice when something changes, protecting the value of the work already invested, and being willing to adjust the next action as new information emerges.**
