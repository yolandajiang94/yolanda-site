I once handled an equipment order for a Canadian university.

Before shipment, the customer had already provided their designated customs broker's contact information, which I passed on to our freight forwarder.

When the shipment arrived in Canada, it ran into a customs clearance issue.

## **Following the Advice, Then Following Up**

Since the freight forwarder already had the customer's broker information, I asked whether they had contacted the broker and whether I still needed to involve the customer.

The freight forwarder told me that the customer needed to contact the local carrier directly and confirm the use of their designated broker.

I checked once more:

_Does the customer need to contact them directly?_

The answer was yes.

So I followed that direction and emailed the customer, asking them to contact FedEx to authorize the use of their designated customs broker.

At the time, this seemed reasonable. I did not know much about international logistics, while the freight forwarder handled shipments every day. They were also a long-term logistics partner of our company, and I was used to working with them in a cooperative and accommodating way. So I trusted their professional judgment.

A few days passed. The shipment was still held in customs, and the customer had not replied.

I followed up again.

Still no progress.

So I sent another reminder.

What confused me at the time was:

**Why wasn't the customer taking action?**

From my perspective, the next step seemed clear. The freight forwarder had told me what the customer needed to do, and I had passed that information on.

## **The Customer's Reply Changed How I Saw the Problem**

Eventually, the customer replied that the shipment had cleared customs and would soon be delivered.

But they also mentioned something I had not expected:

_“It seems that the broker was put as the recipient in the FedEx form instead of me. That caused the confusion and delays.”_

Until then, I had understood the delay mainly as a matter of the customer not yet taking the action I had asked them to take. Their reply suggested that there had also been confusion in how the shipping information was handled.

I still do not know exactly what went wrong in the FedEx or customs process, or which action ultimately resolved the issue.

So I went back through the earlier messages. The customer had provided their customs broker's information well in advance, and I had already passed it to the freight forwarder.

More importantly, when the clearance issue first appeared, I had actually asked:

_Have you contacted the customer's customs broker? Do I still need to contact the customer?_

The question had occurred to me, and I had even asked it.

But once the freight forwarder gave me a clear answer, I stopped questioning the process. My attention shifted from “Is this the right way to handle the issue?” to “How do I get the customer to take action?”

## **Where I Could Have Handled It Better**

Looking back, trusting the freight forwarder's expertise was not the problem. I needed their guidance on a logistics issue I did not fully understand.

What I could have done better was to revisit the original assumption when repeated follow-ups led nowhere, rather than continuing to push the customer toward the same action.

**When something repeatedly fails to move forward, don't just follow up again. Go back and check whether what you are pushing for is actually the right next step.**
