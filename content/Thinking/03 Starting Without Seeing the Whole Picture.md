I have always had a tendency to make things feel more complicated before I even begin. Especially when it is important, or when past experience tells me that it may not be easy to handle, I can quickly get the sense that certain parts may be difficult or require a lot of back-and-forth. I may not know exactly what the difficulties are yet, but I already expect it to be a lot of work.

Over time, I began to realize that **sometimes the complexity does not come entirely from the task itself. I form an expectation of how difficult it will be before I have really understood it.** Once I start checking the information and dealing with the actual issues, things often turn out to be more manageable than they first seemed. Much of my hesitation around complex tasks happens before I actually begin.

## **Complex Customer Requirements**

Over the past five years, complex customer requirements have often made me hesitate.

Sometimes a customer provided specifications, but the actual application was unclear. Sometimes the requirement went beyond our standard products, and I had to figure out whether we were missing information, whether the product was not a good fit, or whether another solution might work.

When I first received a request like this, I often did not know the answer. But once I actually started working on it, I did not need to know the complete solution immediately. I could first clarify what the customer was trying to do, identify what was already clear and what information was still missing, and then take specific questions back to the customer or discuss them internally.

As the missing information gradually came in, the vague problem would turn into several specific problems I could actually deal with.

## **When a Problem Feels Bigger Before I Even Start**

I saw the same pattern again when I was preparing for job interviews.

I needed to organize several past project cases for interviews, but I put it off for almost a month. The projects most worth talking about were often the ones that had been the most complicated to handle. The thought of going back through all those details and reconstructing the whole process made me instinctively reluctant to start.

Then one day, I simply opened GPT and wrote down as many facts and details as I could remember. It helped me organize them into the STAR structure, and I quickly had a first version. Later, when I wanted to use those cases for my digital garden, I went back to verify the facts and reconstruct the timelines more carefully. But for the interviews at the time, the first version was already enough.

The contrast was even clearer when I prepared my résumé.

I joined my previous company in July 2021 and left in July 2026. After five years away from the job market, I was looking for work again at 32, with nine years of experience. Instinctively, I felt that this résumé had to be very good. But I had no clear idea what “very good” actually meant. The more important I thought it was, the less I knew where to begin, so I delayed it for several days.

Once I started, it was much simpler than I had imagined. On the first day, I spent about an hour organizing the timeline and main responsibilities from my previous jobs. The next day, I spent another hour or so finding a suitable template, putting the content into it, and making some adjustments. That was basically it. **There was only so much I could do with a résumé.** 

## **Problems Without Natural Boundaries**

Not everything has a clear finish line like a résumé or a STAR case.

Spoken English has been a challenge for me for many years. Since finishing the college entrance exam in 2013, I have tried many different ways to improve my English. After I started working, my reading, emails, and work-related English continued to improve, but speaking remained difficult. For years, “improving my spoken English” was a huge and vague goal.

At the same time, I felt that after so many years in overseas sales and now being in my thirties, I should not still be using such simple English. The result was that simple expressions did not feel good enough, while more complicated ones were difficult to use in real time.

This year, I gradually started letting go of “speaking English fluently” as the goal. What I actually need now is to handle the situations that repeatedly come up in overseas sales, including overseas business trips, video calls, and factory visits. Compared with the endless goal of “improving my English,” these specific situations helped me see for the first time that what I actually need to learn can be finite.

Later, when a customer might visit, I prepared some possible topics in advance. Many of the expressions AI gave me were very simple. In the past, I might have thought: How can English this simple be enough for an adult who has been working for years?

But this time, I started thinking: maybe it really can be this simple. I can understand it, and I might actually be able to say it.

My digital garden showed me another change. I am willing to read and learn the English articles on my own website because they are about my work, my experiences, and things I genuinely want to express.

In the past, I often learned English materials first, hoping I might use them someday. Now the order is sometimes reversed: **I already have something I want to say, and then I learn how to say it in English.**

My spoken English is still a work in progress, but I now have a clearer idea of what I actually need to learn.

## **When the Final Boundary Is Unknown**

English at least has a relatively clear direction. I know that I work in overseas sales, and ultimately I want my spoken English to serve real work.

Long-term career development is much more open.

After 35 or 40, will I continue working in sales for a company, or will I gradually build more abilities and options that do not depend on any one company?

In the past, when I thought about questions like this, I often found myself asking, “What should I ultimately do?” I wanted to figure out the future first and then decide what I should do today.

But there is no single correct answer to long-term career development. The time frame may be five years, ten years, or even longer. I cannot plan all the way to the end from where I am today.

Now I am more willing to ask:

What can I start today that will still be meaningful even if I don't know where I will eventually end up, and that I can continue building over time?

I like the idea of a **connection point**. It is not a final answer or a planned ten-year career path. It is something I can start now, continue building over time, and carry with me between where I am today and a future that is still unclear.

For me, real industrial B2B experience, English for actual work situations, my digital garden, and skills I may continue developing around websites, SEO, or other areas can all become connection points at different stages.

## **Dealing With the Problem That Actually Exists**

Looking back at these problems of very different scales, I am learning to start without needing to understand the whole problem first.

Some boundaries only appear after I start. Some need to be defined by me. And for some long-term questions, there is simply no way to know the endpoint yet.

I don't need to deal with the entire task I have imagined. I only need to deal with the problem that actually exists in front of me. For questions that do not have answers yet, I can take the next step, let reality give me new information, and decide from there.
