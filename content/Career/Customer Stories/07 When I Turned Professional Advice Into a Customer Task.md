
I once handled an equipment order for a Canadian university.

Before shipment, the customer had already told me that they had their own customs broker and shared the broker's contact information with me. **I provided this information to our freight forwarder when arranging the shipment.**

When the shipment arrived in Canada, it ran into a customs clearance issue.

**Since the freight forwarder already had the customer's broker information, I asked whether they had contacted the broker and whether I still needed to involve the customer.**

The freight forwarder told me that the customer needed to contact the local carrier directly and confirm the use of their designated broker.

I checked once more:

Does the customer need to contact them directly?

The answer was yes.

So I followed that direction and emailed the customer.

At the time, this seemed reasonable. I did not know much about international logistics, while the freight forwarder handled shipments every day. **They were also a long-term logistics partner of our company, and I was used to working with them in a cooperative and accommodating way**. So when they gave me a clear direction, I naturally trusted their professional judgment and focused on getting the customer to follow it.

## **When Nothing Moved, I Kept Following Up**

A few days passed. The shipment was still held in customs, and the customer had not replied.

I followed up again.

Still no progress.

So I sent another reminder.

What confused me at the time was:

**Why wasn't the customer taking action?**

From my perspective, the process seemed clear. The freight forwarder had told me what the customer needed to do, and I had passed that information on.

So when nothing moved, my response was to follow up again.

What I did not do was go back and ask:

**Is the action I am asking the customer to take actually the right next step?**

## **The Customer's Reply Changed How I Saw the Problem**

Eventually, the customer replied that the shipment had cleared customs and would soon be delivered.

But they also mentioned something none of us had expected:

“It seems that the broker was put as the recipient in the FedEx form instead of me. That caused the confusion and delays.”

This changed how I saw the situation.

Until then, I had understood the delay mainly as a matter of the customer not yet taking the action I had asked them to take.

But their reply suggested that there had also been some unexpected confusion in how the shipping information was handled.

I still do not know exactly what went wrong in the FedEx or customs process, or at which point the information became confused. What became clear was that the situation was more complicated than simply **“the customer had not done what was required.”**

So I went back through the earlier messages.

The customer had provided their customs broker's information well in advance, and I had already passed it to the freight forwarder.

More importantly, I noticed something in my own earlier message.

When the clearance issue first appeared, I had actually asked:

Have you contacted the customer's customs broker? Do I still need to contact the customer?

I had already sensed that, since the customer had their own customs broker, perhaps that should be the first direction to check.

The question had occurred to me, and I had even asked it.

But once the freight forwarder gave me a clear answer, I stopped questioning the process. My attention shifted from **“Is this the right way to handle the issue?”** to **“How do I get the customer to take action?”**

## **Where I Could Have Handled It Better**

Looking back, trusting the freight forwarder's expertise was not the problem. Asking someone with more experience about a logistics issue I did not understand was exactly what I should have done.

What I could have done better was **not turn that professional advice into a customer task so quickly.**

When several follow-ups led nowhere, I could have gone back and checked the original assumption instead of simply sending another reminder.

My pattern had become:

**Receive an instruction → Pass it to the customer → No action → Follow up again.**

The shipment was eventually delivered without serious consequences.

But the experience left me with a simple reminder:

**When something repeatedly fails to move forward, don't just follow up again. Go back and check whether what you are pushing for is actually the right next step.**
