# **Internal Closure and the Freedom to Move Forward**

For a long time, I believed that a problem was not truly finished until it was fully understood.

If someone misunderstood me, I felt there was still something unfinished.

If someone saw only one side of a situation, I felt the need to provide the complete picture.

I wanted the other person to understand what really happened.

I wanted my intentions to be recognized.

I wanted the story to have the “correct” ending.

Only then could I feel at peace.

But over time, I realized that this way of seeking closure was exhausting me.

---

## **The Need to Complete the Story**

I have always been someone who wants things to make sense.

I like clear conclusions.

I dislike unfinished situations.

When something happens, I naturally want to analyze:

What exactly happened?

Why did it happen?

What did each person mean?

What is the most accurate interpretation?

This ability helps me understand situations deeply.

But it also created a hidden expectation:

I believed that if I explained enough, the other person would eventually arrive at the same understanding.

I thought closure came from reaching agreement.

---

## **But Understanding Is Not Completely Under My Control**

One of the hardest lessons I have learned is this:

Other people understand the world through their own experiences.

Even if I explain something clearly, they may still interpret it differently.

Not because my explanation is wrong.

Not because they do not care.

Simply because everyone has their own perspective.

I cannot completely control how another person understands me.

And trying to control that process consumes a tremendous amount of energy.

---

## **Taking Back the Right to Close the Loop**

I am learning a different way of creating closure.

Closure does not always mean:

“I explained everything.”

Closure does not always mean:

“The other person finally agrees with me.”

Sometimes closure means:

“I understand what happened.”

“I understand my own position.”

“I know what I experienced.”

“And I choose not to continue spending energy on this.”

This is what I call internal closure.

It means bringing the ability to finish a story back to myself.

---

## **Stopping Is Not Giving Up**

In the past, stopping a conversation felt like losing.

It felt like:

“Maybe I did not explain myself well enough.”

“Maybe the other person will keep misunderstanding me.”

“Maybe I should say one more thing.”

But now I am learning:

Choosing to stop does not mean I agree with everything.

It does not mean I give up my perspective.

It does not mean I lose my right to express myself.

Sometimes stopping is simply a judgment about timing and energy.

Sometimes continuing the conversation will not create more understanding.

It will only create more damage.

In those moments, stopping is not surrender.

It is a choice.

---

## **A New Definition of Closure**

I used to think closure meant:

“I have said everything I wanted to say.”

Now I think closure means:

“I have understood myself clearly enough to move forward.”

Some things will be fully understood.

Some things will only be partially understood.

Some things may remain different perspectives.

But life can still continue.

A healthy relationship with others does not require complete agreement on everything.

And a healthy relationship with myself does not require everyone to fully understand me.

---

## **Bringing My Energy Back**

Over the past year, I have started learning a different way of relating to uncertainty.

I can communicate honestly.

I can care about other people's feelings.

I can explain when explanation is useful.

But I no longer want my peace to depend on whether someone reaches the exact same understanding as me.

The ability to close a chapter does not have to come from another person.

Sometimes, the most important conversation is the one I have with myself.

I can acknowledge what happened.

I can respect my own experience.

And I can choose to move forward.
