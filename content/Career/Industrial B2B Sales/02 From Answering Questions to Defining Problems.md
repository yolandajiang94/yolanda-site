
_Why solving a customer problem sometimes starts with deciding whether the problem has been defined correctly_

For many years in industrial B2B sales, I had a simple instinct when a customer reported a problem:

**If the customer has a problem, I should help solve it.**

If I could not answer a technical question myself, I would go to after-sales or R&D. If the first explanation did not fully answer the customer's concern, I would keep asking. As long as the customer still had a question, I felt the issue was not finished.

There was good reason for this instinct. Sales is often the customer's main connection to the company, and pushing internal resources to help customers is part of the job.

But one case made me realize that something was missing from this logic:

**A customer raising a problem does not necessarily mean that a product problem has already been established.**

## **A Question I Found Difficult to Stop Asking**

A research customer was using eight units of the same equipment. Two of them appeared to behave abnormally at very low current.

The customer had been testing at 2 μA and 4 μA. Her question was compelling:

**If all eight units were the same model, why did only these two behave differently?**

My first reaction was similar to hers. I worried that the two units might indeed have a problem, so I went to after-sales and R&D for an explanation.

This was exactly how I had handled customer issues for years. If the customer had a question I could not explain, I wanted to find someone who could. If the answer was incomplete, I would keep pushing.

I wanted to get to the bottom of it.

Then I went back to the product specifications.

The lowest current range was:

**5 μA–1 mA.**

The 2 μA and 4 μA tests were already below that range. This was not a limitation introduced after the problem appeared; the range had been clearly provided during the quotation stage.

At the same time, although the customer reported that two units behaved abnormally while the other six did not, **we did not yet have the raw test data behind that comparison.**

Her concern was real. But with the tests already outside the specified range and without the underlying data for comparison, the conclusion — **these two units are faulty** — was not yet supported by the evidence we had.

## **The Hard Part Was Stopping**

Looking back, the technical boundary seems straightforward.

The minimum was 5 μA. The customer was testing below it. Ask her to return to the specified range and test again.

But at the time, stopping there was surprisingly difficult for me.

The customer's original question was still unanswered:

**Why did only two of eight identical units behave differently?**

My old instinct was to keep going back to R&D until someone could explain that difference, even if it occurred outside the specified range.

For years, I had associated this persistence with being responsible to the customer.

This time, however, I began to see another responsibility: **before continuing an investigation, I needed to be clear about what the available evidence actually supported.**

The tests were outside the specified operating range, and we did not yet have the raw data needed to verify the reported difference between the eight units. At that point, the more useful next step was not to keep searching for an explanation, but to ask the customer to test again within the specified range.

A customer having a question and the company having an established product problem to investigate are not always the same thing.

My job was not only to push a problem forward.

I also had to decide whether the problem, as currently defined, had enough evidence behind it.

## **Setting a Boundary**

I eventually wrote back to the customer and explained that the lowest current range was **5 μA–1 mA**, and that the 2 μA test was below the specified range. I also noted that this specification had been provided earlier.

Instead of continuing to promise an explanation for the behavior at 2 μA, I asked her to test again within the specified range.

It was a simple email, but for me it was a difficult decision.

For the first time, I was consciously telling myself:

**An unanswered question does not always mean that the same investigation should continue in the same direction.**

Sometimes the evidence only allows the problem to go so far.

## **When the Evidence Changed, So Did the Problem**

The customer was not upset by the boundary.

She tested again.

This time, she still observed abnormal behavior at **5 μA, 6 μA, and 7 μA** — within the specified range. More importantly, for the first time, she provided the raw test data exported from the system, which gave R&D actual data to analyze.

Now the situation was different. The abnormal behavior was within the specified range, and there was actual data to support further investigation.

**The problem had become something we could investigate further.**

So we reopened it and continued the technical analysis.

The later troubleshooting is another story. What mattered to me was that stopping the first investigation had not prevented us from helping the customer.

The evidence changed, so our response changed with it.

## **Defining the Problem Is Part of Sales**

This experience changed how I think about customer problems.

In the past, when a customer said:

“The equipment has a problem.”

my instinct was to ask:

**How do we solve it?**

Now I try to ask one question first:

**What problem has actually been established?**

A customer's observation deserves attention. But an observation, an interpretation, and a confirmed product problem are not the same thing.

Good customer service still means taking concerns seriously and pushing internally when needed. What changed for me was the belief that every unanswered question must be pursued until the company produces an answer.

Sometimes good judgment means continuing to push.

Sometimes it means recognizing:

**Based on the evidence we have now, we need better information before taking the investigation further.**

And when better evidence appears, we can open it again.

For me, that is also part of defining the problem — and part of the salesperson's responsibility.
