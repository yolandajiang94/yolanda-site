# From Learning Sales Techniques to Understanding the Profession

_Rethinking What Business Development Means After Five Years in Industrial B2B Sales_

## When Sales Was Mostly About Execution

For several years, my understanding of business development was relatively straightforward.

Find the right companies.

Identify the right contacts.

Write a good cold email.

Follow up consistently.

Wait for opportunities.

This was the system I relied on for most of my customer development work.

I spent a lot of time improving the details.

I learned how to screen companies more carefully, how to identify relevant roles, how to write clearer emails, and how to follow up over a long period of time.

None of these skills were wrong.

They were useful, and they remain part of my work today.

But after several years in industrial B2B sales, I gradually realized that I had been learning individual sales techniques without fully understanding the profession behind them.

---

## Cold Outreach Is Not the Same as Business Development

For a long time, I unconsciously treated cold outreach as the center of customer development.

My assumption was simple:

If I could find more suitable companies, write better emails, and follow up more consistently, I would create more opportunities.

But a highly relevant company is not necessarily an opportunity.

A company may fit the ideal customer profile perfectly and still have no reason to buy today.

It may already have a supplier.

It may have no current project.

Its budget may have been frozen.

The timing may simply be wrong.

This made me realize that customer relevance and business opportunity are two different things.

A better question is not only:

**"Is this the right company?"**

It is also:

**"Why might this company need something now?"**

That shift changed the way I began to think about business development.

---

## Learning to Look for Opportunities, Not Just Companies

Once I started thinking beyond customer lists, I became more interested in what was happening around a company.

Is it expanding?

Is it developing a new product?

Is it hiring engineers?

Is it entering a new market?

Is the industry around it growing?

These signals do not guarantee an order.

But they provide context.

They help explain why one company may be worth approaching now while another equally relevant company may not be.

This was also when I began to understand the importance of market awareness.

For years, I had focused heavily on individual customers.

But sales does not happen in isolation.

Markets change.

Industries shift.

Demand moves between countries and customer groups.

A salesperson needs to notice those changes rather than continue using the same development pattern simply because it worked before.

---

## Understanding the Value of Industry Connections

Another blind spot in my earlier work was industry networking.

Most of my attention went directly to end users: engineers, researchers, lab managers, and other potential buyers.

I paid much less attention to the wider network around an industry.

Distributors.

Consultants.

Integrators.

Suppliers.

Other salespeople.

Engineers and project managers who might never directly purchase from me.

Earlier in my career, advice such as "get to know more people in the industry" sounded vague to me.

I understood the words, but not the practical value behind them.

Over time, I began to see that opportunities do not always start with a sales email.

Sometimes they come from information.

Sometimes from an introduction.

Sometimes from someone remembering you months later.

Sometimes simply from being connected closely enough to an industry to notice what is changing.

An industry network is not separate from business development.

It is part of business development.

---

## Knowing the Value of Networking Was Not Enough

This realization also made me look differently at trade shows, business trips, and customer visits.

I had always known that these activities were valuable.

I had even actively looked for opportunities to participate in them.

But knowing their value and knowing how to use them effectively were two different things.

As a naturally introverted person, I was comfortable communicating when there was already a clear business context.

Starting conversations with strangers was much harder.

At events, it was easy for me to stay on the side, observe, and tell myself that there were not many relevant people to talk to.

Looking back, I realized that the limitation was not the event itself.

I had never developed my own way of participating.

This was an important distinction.

I did not need to become the most outgoing person in the room.

But I did need to learn how to create connections in a way that worked for me.

---

## Building a Sales Approach Around My Strengths

For a long time, I sometimes interpreted this discomfort as a weakness in sales.

Later, I started looking at my strengths more carefully.

I may not naturally enjoy high-frequency socializing or aggressive prospecting.

But I am comfortable with long-term communication.

I am patient with customers.

I learn technical products relatively quickly.

I can stay engaged with a customer over a long sales cycle.

And once a relationship is established, I am good at maintaining trust.

This helped me understand that becoming better at sales does not necessarily mean becoming a completely different type of person.

It means building a more complete system around the strengths I already have while deliberately improving the areas that limit me.

For me, that means becoming better at identifying opportunities, understanding markets, building industry connections, and creating more ways for potential customers to enter my network.

---

## From Managing Customers to Building an Opportunity Network

The biggest change in my thinking is that I no longer see business development as a linear process:

Find a customer.

Send an email.

Get a reply.

Close an order.

A more realistic picture is a network.

A potential customer may become an opportunity months later.

An engineer may move to another company.

A distributor may introduce a project.

A conversation at an industry event may lead to another connection.

A previous customer may return with a new requirement.

A company that has no project today may become highly relevant after its business changes.

Seen this way, the job is not only to develop individual customers.

It is to gradually build an **opportunity network**.

Cold email still has a place in that system.

So do LinkedIn, Google research, trade shows, customer visits, referrals, industry relationships, and content.

No single channel is the whole system.

---

## What I Want to Become Better At

After this reflection, the skills I want to develop are different from the ones I focused on earlier in my career.

I still care about writing good emails and communicating professionally.

But I also want to become better at:

Understanding markets.

Recognizing opportunity signals.

Building industry connections.

Using multiple channels to reach customers.

Maintaining long-term professional relationships.

Making better commercial judgments.

These abilities are harder to learn than a cold email template.

They cannot be mastered by reading a few examples.

They develop through years of observing markets, talking to people, working on projects, making mistakes, and reflecting on what actually creates opportunities.

---

## Reflection

For years, I was learning sales techniques.

How to find customers.

How to write emails.

How to follow up.

How to communicate.

Those skills gave me a foundation.

But only later did I begin to understand the profession itself.

Industrial B2B sales is not simply about finding more people to contact.

It is about understanding where opportunities come from, recognizing when markets and customers are changing, building relationships across an industry, and gradually developing the judgment to know where to invest time and attention.

That shift did not give me a perfect sales system.

It gave me something more useful:

a broader understanding of what I still need to learn.

And I think that is the beginning of a different stage in my career.
