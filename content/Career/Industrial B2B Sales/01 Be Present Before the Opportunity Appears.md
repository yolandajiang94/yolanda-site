
_Why consistent customer development matters even when nothing seems to be happening_

Much of proactive B2B sales is not particularly exciting.

You identify potential customers, keep records, send an outreach email, and hear nothing back. Some time later, you reach out again.

Most of these actions do not immediately lead to an inquiry, let alone an order.

The customer may not have an active project. There may be no budget yet. Or they may simply have no need for new equipment at that moment. With long-cycle industrial products, salespeople have limited control over when a customer actually enters a buying window.

That does not make the work before the opportunity appears unnecessary.

After years of doing proactive sales development, I have come to see much of the job in simpler terms:

**Find the right customers. Keep a reliable record. Stay in touch with a reasonable rhythm. And when an opportunity finally appears, make sure you are still there.**

## **Deciding Who Is Worth Developing**

Toward the end of 2025, I started reorganizing my customer database and building a more focused list of target accounts.

It is easy to fall into a simple pattern in proactive sales:

Find a prospect, send an email, get no reply, and move on to the next one.

But as my database grew, I realized that constantly adding new contacts was not enough.

I needed to answer a different question:

**Which customers are genuinely relevant enough to be worth contacting again?**

In the academic market, I screened research groups based on their research direction and potential applications, focusing on areas such as battery technology, electrochemistry, energy materials, and energy storage.

Good targeting cannot tell me who will buy.

A professor whose research is highly relevant to our products may have no purchasing plan today. Another laboratory may receive funding or start a new project several months later.

So customer selection does not answer:

Who is ready to buy now?

It answers:

**Who is worth keeping in my development pipeline?**

Those are different questions.

## **Keeping the Right Customers in View**

Once I identified a relevant prospect, I kept the development history and stayed in touch over time.

That did not mean repeatedly sending the same email. Different contacts were spaced out and approached with different content.

If a customer was genuinely relevant to our products and applications, a lack of response did not automatically mean I should remove them from my development list.

Sometimes the right thing to do was simply to keep the record and show up again at an appropriate time.

## **An Opportunity That Appeared Months Later**

One day, a professor at a Korean university replied to one of my emails.

His response went directly into a real purchasing need. His lab was considering purchasing new testing channels and expected to continue expanding its testing capacity.

He copied one of his lab members into the email, asked him to discuss the detailed requirements with me, and said he hoped I could provide a good offer.

After receiving the message, I looked back at my development history with this customer.

It was not the first time I had contacted him.

The first outreach had been on January 27, 2026.

No reply.

The second was on March 2.

Still no reply.

The email he responded to, on May 21, was my third outreach.

The three emails were not repetitions of the same message. They were separate contacts over time with different content.

In the May email, I happened to mention that if his group planned to expand testing capacity, add new channels, or evaluate new setups in the future, I would be happy to provide information when needed.

His reply was strikingly close to that context: they were considering purchasing new channels and would continue expanding their cycler capacity.

From there, I worked directly with his lab member to clarify the testing requirements and system configuration. The communication moved smoothly, and the project eventually became an order.

## **What Does No Response Actually Mean?**

Looking back at those three contacts, what interests me most is not that “the third email finally worked.”

It is a more basic question:

**What did the first two non-responses actually mean?**

The truth is, I do not know.

I do not know whether the professor carefully read the earlier emails, or whether those previous contacts created any familiarity with me or with the company.

So I would not describe this case as:

I followed up three times, built trust, and won the order.

Industrial B2B sales rarely gives us such clean cause-and-effect relationships.

What I can say is much simpler.

He was a prospect I had screened and considered relevant to our products and applications. He did not respond to the first two contacts. By the time of the third, he had entered a real purchasing window.

That was enough to reinforce something important for me:

**No response is not the same as no potential.**

A customer not replying tells me that I did not receive a response at that particular moment.

It does not automatically tell me that the customer is a poor fit.

## **Customer Fit and Customer Timing Are Different Things**

In proactive sales, it is easy to judge prospects through immediate feedback.

A reply feels like validation. Silence can feel like a sign that the customer is not interested.

But in long-cycle industrial B2B sales, I have learned to separate two questions.

Is this customer genuinely relevant to what we sell?

That is **customer fit**.

Does this customer currently have a project, budget, or purchasing plan?

That is **customer timing**.

A customer can be a strong fit and still have the wrong timing.

The professor did not reply in January. He still did not reply in March. But when a real purchasing need appeared later, it confirmed that my original decision to keep him as a target customer had not been unreasonable.

The missing piece had been timing.

If the process is always:

Find a new customer → Send one email → No reply → Move on

then it becomes very easy to confuse **wrong timing** with **wrong customer**.

A simple and reliable development system helps keep those two things separate.

## **The Basic Work Before an Opportunity Exists**

There is nothing particularly remarkable about this case.

I did not change the customer's decision through a brilliant negotiation. I did not use a special sales technique to create demand that did not already exist.

The lab needed more testing capacity because of its own research and development.

What I did was much more ordinary:

**Screen. Record. Reach out. Receive no response. Show up again later.**

This is basic proactive sales work.

And much of the time, there is no visible result on the day the work is done.

That used to be difficult for me to evaluate. It is natural to look at immediate results: How many people replied? Did anyone ask for a quotation? Did any real projects appear?

Those metrics still matter.

But not every useful development action produces an immediate response.

A well-matched prospect may simply have no project or budget today. More outreach cannot create a need that does not exist, just as repeatedly contacting a poor-fit customer will not turn them into a good prospect.

What I can control is more limited:

**Am I targeting the right customers?**

**Am I keeping the good ones in my development system?**

**Am I staying in touch with a reasonable rhythm?**

The timing of the customer's actual need belongs largely to the customer.

That is why I no longer see proactive sales simply as a process of creating opportunities.

A large part of it is quieter than that.

It is doing the basic work before an opportunity exists — and making sure that when the customer's timing finally changes, I have not disappeared too early.

**Target the right customers.**  
**Keep a reliable record.**  
**Stay in touch with a reasonable rhythm.**  
**Be present when the opportunity appears.**
